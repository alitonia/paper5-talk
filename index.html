Can we prove a model is safe
before we train it?

When you fine-tune a language model on prompts collected from other people, some of those prompts may be written by an attacker. In this talk I show a method that computes, before the training step runs, a worst-case bound on how far poisoned prompts in the batch can push the model's answers.

A batch of 20 instruction prompts. The filled squares are prompts an attacker replaced. The question is what those squares can and cannot do to the model's answers.

Three questions.

  • What actually happens when a language model is fine-tuned on a batch of prompts? I follow one training step on a real batch of 20 prompts.
  • What can an attacker do through a training prompt? We look at what the attacker controls and what a successful attack would look like.
  • How can we bound the damage before training starts, and how well does that bound hold up when it is stress-tested and audited?

The first two parts are background and the method starts at slide 8. Every number in this talk is real and comes from recorded runs.

What is being fine-tuned, and on what data.

A language model that has finished pretraining predicts the next word for any text, but it does not follow instructions well. Instruction tuning fixes that by training on short prompts together with good answers. The batch I will use for the whole talk is a real one: 20 short prompts, fixed in the code.

PROMPT 1 OF 20
What is the capital of France?
PROMPT 4 OF 20
What is 17 times 8?
PROMPT 20 OF 20
Summarize the plot of Hamlet in one sentence.
Why this matters

Prompts like these are collected at scale: scraped from the web, donated, or bought from crowd workers, and nobody checks the people who write them. Instruction tuning is the stage where the model's behavior is shaped most directly by data the operator did not write.

What one training step does to the model.

During one step, the model reads each prompt and produces an answer. We measure how wrong each answer is, and then every trainable number is adjusted a tiny amount so the error goes down. The direction of the adjustment is called the gradient, and that word comes back several times.

It helps to think of a prompt as more than text. A prompt is also an instruction to move the model's parameters, and whoever wrote it chose the direction of that push. The attack in a moment works through exactly this mechanism.

LoRA: we train small patches and freeze everything else.

Adjusting all 1.5 billion numbers during tuning would be expensive, and for my purposes it would be hopeless to analyze. So in practice everyone uses LoRA: the model is frozen completely, and a small trainable patch is attached to each attention block. Only the patches are trained.

This choice is what makes the analysis feasible. Any push that a prompt can make on the model ends up inside those 4.36 million patch numbers. That space is small enough that we can compute in it exactly, while the model itself stays a full 1.5-billion-parameter network.

The patches fly in one at a time and the lock drops onto the frozen part. For people who know LoRA: rank 16, alpha 32, on the q, k, v and o projections. Say the ratio out loud: under a third of one percent trainable.

What the attacker controls.

The attacker may replace up to n of the 20 prompts with text of their choosing. The replacement looks like an ordinary prompt, but its training signal is built to damage a specific answer elsewhere in the batch.

the batch with n = 4 poisoned, as an example

Why this is hard

We have to be safe against every choice of slots and every crafted text in the attack family. Running one attack and checking the result tells us about that attack only, so testing alone cannot give a guarantee.

How we measure a single answer: the margin.

At the position where the model answers, it gives every word in its vocabulary a score, usually called a logit. The answer is the word with the highest score. The gap between the best and the second-best word is the margin of that answer.

If an attack pushes a margin below zero, the answer flips. So the method protects one quantity: it keeps every margin positive, whatever the poisoned prompts do.

The question we set out to answer.

Certified radius n*

The largest number n* with this property: for every possible choice of n* poisoned prompts, and for every crafted text in the attack family, all 20 margins stay positive after the one training step.

The word "every" appears twice, and that is the hard part. Testing a few attacks tells us about those attacks. To say something in advance about all of them, we cannot rely on running training at all.

The way around it: stop reasoning about the texts, and reason about the pushes they cause in the 4.36-million-dimensional patch space. A push is a vector, and a vector can be bounded with algebra.

A few of the 1,048,575 possible attack placements. The guarantee has to cover all of them, down to the last row.

You can compare it with braking distance in a car: how far the car travels after you brake, plus an allowance for the road bending under you.

First term, the straight push

I is the worst possible alignment, over the whole batch, between the gradient of a protected answer and the gradient of any allowed attack prompt. Multiplied by the learning rate η and the poisoned fraction n/B, it gives how far the margin can be pushed in a straight line.

Second term, the bend

The push also bends as the parameters move. L bounds that bending, F is the largest attack-gradient size in the batch, and the factor 2 doubles L as a safety margin. At our learning rate this term is small, but it grows with the square of the learning rate.

The trick behind the exact number.

Take one protected answer. Its worst case needs the alignment between its gradient and every attack gradient in the batch. The obvious way is pair by pair: compute both gradients and take their dot product, for every pair. With 20 prompts that is 400 pairs, and the count grows with the square of the batch size.

Instead, we push the protected answer's gradient through the model as a direction, in a single Jacobian-vector product. One pass returns its alignment with every prompt in the batch at once. The network itself does the pairing.

Dgₚ gapq how much answer q's gap moves when nudged along gₚ
=
⟨ ∇gapq , gₚ ⟩ the dot product of the two pushes — their alignment

A dot product is the alignment: positive pushes the same way, negative pushes against. We take the largest over all q, and one pass hands us all of them. The direction lives in the LoRA patch space, so the frozen 1.54 billion parameters are never touched — no Hessian, exact, 11 seconds for everything.

We also ran the attack for real.

The bound is a worst-case statement, and worst-case statements can hide implementation bugs, so we also executed the attack end to end and compared every answer before and after. Each check compares against both the clean step and the untouched model, which also catches a case the margin cannot see on its own: a third word overtaking both leaders.

Testing it more widely: 38 more configurations.

The same procedure was re-run across 38 configurations: batches of 50 and 100 prompts, adapter ranks 4, 8 and 32, learning rates up to 6×10⁻⁴, and two more models, Qwen2.5-7B and Mistral-7B.

  • The Qwen family keeps the radius saturated up to learning rate 4×10⁻⁴, and batches up to 100 prompts certify fully
  • The 7B model certifies 20 out of 20 in 53 seconds
  • Mistral-7B at 4.5×10⁻⁴ was the one failure: a certified prompt actually flipped. Across 2,928 replay checks over the whole grid, this was the single violation

The flip is not a contradiction. The bound holds only if I, F and L are valid for the model at hand, and Mistral's constants are much harder: influence about 39 times larger than Qwen's, curvature about 18 times larger, so its certified regime ends at a learning rate roughly 33 times lower, and the flip happened about 36 times beyond that. The certificate shrank exactly where its inputs got worse, which raises an obvious question: were those inputs honest in the first place? That is the next slide.

Checking our own two assumptions.

Assumption 1: attacks stay inside the measured envelope

This held. On six models we evaluated every one of the 220−1 = 1,048,575 possible poisoned subsets, exactly, using a matrix identity that makes the enumeration cheap. The worst damage ratio was 0.84, and it always occurred with a single poisoned prompt: when several poisons are present, their pushes partially cancel.

Assumption 2: the measured L covers curvature on attack directions

This failed on all six models. True curvature along attack directions is 63 to 8,391 times the measured value. The chart shows what happens to the radius when it is recomputed with the true curvature.

L was the one number we measured rather than computed exactly, and the audit asks what that measurement is worth on the model's own attack directions instead of random ones. The answer is not the one we hoped for, but at least it is a precise number, and we report the failure and the survival together.

What this work does not claim.

  • One training step. The certificate covers a single full-batch update. If the constants are held fixed across steps, the tightest margin would be used up after about 4 steps, so an attacker spreading poison over many steps is outside the guarantee.
  • One attack family. The bound charges attacks that push the top answer toward its runner-up. Free-form adversarial losses are not covered, and the family is stated wherever the guarantee is stated.
  • One audited constant. L is measured with a safety factor, not derived from the architecture. The audit quantifies how far to trust it on each model.
Where this goes next

The same bound composes across several training steps once the constants are made to track the model as it moves, and the framework extends to other adapter families and broader attack families. That extension is the next part of this research line, and it is already in progress.

Summary.

  • A prompt is a push on the model's parameters. When the training data comes from other people, those pushes are chosen by other people, and that is what makes instruction tuning risky.
  • The push can be bounded before training. Exact worst-case alignment inside the LoRA patch space, plus a safety-factored curvature term, gives a certified radius of 20 out of 20 prompts on Qwen2.5-1.5B in 34.8 seconds, and an executed attack with zero flips in 60 checks.
  • The conditions behind the guarantee can be audited. Checking all 1,048,575 subsets on each of six models showed that one assumption holds and one fails, and both the surviving and the collapsing cases are reported.

Every number in this talk came from a recorded run, and the arithmetic on slide 12 can be recomputed from the logs.
